You are not your user. You know how your product works, so you can't see it the way a newcomer does. **User research** closes that gap.

## Start with the problem

Before designing screens, find out who the people are, what they're trying to do, and what gets in their way. A short conversation with a handful of people often reveals more than a week of guessing.

Ask about what people did, not what they would do:

- Better: "Tell me about the last time you booked a train ticket."
- Worse: "Would you use an app that books train tickets?"

People are poor at predicting their own behavior, but good at describing what happened.

## Usability testing

Give someone a realistic task, like "Find a train to Leeds tomorrow morning", and watch them try it with your design. Ask them to think aloud. Don't help, and don't explain the design: where they hesitate or get stuck is exactly what you need to fix.

Testing with **about five people** finds most of the big problems in a design. Fix them, then test again with new people.

## Test early and cheaply

You don't need a finished product. Paper sketches and clickable prototypes reveal problems while they're still cheap to fix.

## Familiar patterns win

People spend most of their time on other sites and apps, so they expect yours to work the same way. This idea is called Jakob's law. A search box, a shopping cart or a menu icon work because people already know them. Be original in what your product does, not in how basic controls behave.

## Keep learning after launch

Watch how many people complete the key task, where they drop out, and which support questions keep coming up. Each one points at the next thing to improve.
